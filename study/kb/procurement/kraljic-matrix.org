#+title: Kraljic and the supplier portfolio
#+summary: Kraljic (1983) classifies purchase categories by strategic importance and difficulty of managing the supply situation into non-critical, leverage, bottleneck and strategic items, each with its own sourcing policy; Olsen and Ellram add relationship analysis and action plans.

* Definition
An *undifferentiated* approach (one static sourcing strategy for everything) is
wrong: items differ in importance and may need ad hoc specifications and
suppliers; strategy must also be dynamic. A *differentiated* approach analyses
all purchased items to choose the ideal relationship → *segmentation of the
supplier portfolio*.
A *portfolio model* simplifies a complex problem by (1) classifying variables
and objects in a matrix and (2) giving strategic recommendations per cell
(Gelderman and Van Weele 2000). Kraljic's unit is the *purchase category*.

* Model
** The two dimensions
| Strategic importance              | Difficulty of supply management         |
|-----------------------------------+-----------------------------------------|
| competence: improves buyer's      | product: novelty, complexity            |
| competences, product quality and  |                                         |
| functionality                     |                                         |
| economic: volume/value, part of a | supply market: few suppliers, supplier  |
| high value-added or profitable    | power, suppliers' technical and         |
| product, leverage for other buys, | commercial skills                       |
| impact on product cost            |                                         |
| image: supplier brand critical,   | environment: risk and uncertainty, high |
| environment/safety relations      | logistic costs, long lead times         |
Factors are weighted to position each category.

** The four categories
| Category     | Imp./diff. | Policy                               | Sourcing   |
|--------------+------------+--------------------------------------+------------|
| Non-critical | low/low    | minimize management costs, simplify, | multi      |
|              |            | standardize, fewer suppliers         |            |
| Leverage     | high/low   | cut purchase price, develop          | dual/multi |
|              |            | collaboration; buyer-dominated       |            |
| Bottleneck   | low/high   | reduce or eliminate, alternatives,   | sole       |
|              |            | make in-house, value analysis        |            |
| Strategic    | high/high  | partnership; supplier as "natural    | single or  |
|              |            | extension of the firm"; co-design    | dual       |
- *Leverage*: high spend, standard quality, many suppliers → the buyer can be
  aggressive, but mutual respect keeps the relation healthy.
- *Non-critical*: periodic replenishment agreements ("vuoto per pieno":
  replace empties with full), catalogues, e-procurement.
- *Bottleneck*: not strategic but hard; standardize, simplify with
  [[id:value-analysis][value analysis]], involve the supplier in design or preferential deals.
- *Strategic*: close relations, supplier involved in product development.

** Olsen and Ellram (1997)
Three-step normative model: (1) analyse purchases (Kraljic); (2) analyse
existing supplier relationships (relative attractiveness, strength of the
relation); (3) develop action plans. The desired relationship may differ from
the one actually available.

* Worked example
Car maker: steel coils (high spend, many mills) → leverage: tender, dual
sourcing. Office stationery → non-critical: one catalogue, e-ordering.
Proprietary sensor from one supplier, low value → bottleneck: buffer stock,
redesign to a standard sensor. Battery cells → strategic: long-term
partnership, joint development, single or dual source.

* Pitfall
The unit is the purchase category, not the supplier label. High spend does not
mean high supply risk. Positions change with technology, demand and supply
markets: reassess.
