#+title: Industrial purchasing and the buying process
#+summary: Industrial (B2B) purchasing is professional, interactive and relationship-based; its six phases go from specifications to supplier evaluation, the first three being strategic and the last three operational.

* Definition
In B2C the buyer is the final consumer; in *B2B* the transaction is between
organizations and the goods/services are used in production. Purchasing is a
*support activity* of Porter's [[id:value-chain][value chain]] that feeds all
primary activities. Every actor adds value: a discounter cannot keep low prices
without competitive suppliers; Apple cannot offer high quality unless its
suppliers and intermediaries guarantee it.

Share of purchases on revenue (Kluge 1996): retail 60–85%, computers 60–80%,
vehicles 60–80%, consumer electronics 50–70%, pharmaceuticals 25–50%, services
10–40%. Typical structure of spend: raw materials and components 50%, capex
18%, merchandise 12%, services 12%, admin 5%, spare parts 3%.

* Model
** B2B vs B2C
| B2B feature                    | Meaning                                    |
|--------------------------------+--------------------------------------------|
| Professional purchasing        | organizations, formality, longer times     |
| Derived demand                 | no direct relation with the end user       |
| Inelastic demand               | low price elasticity in the short run      |
| Large orders                   | large sums of money                        |
| Deep product knowledge         | rational approach                          |
| Few, concentrated customers    | industrial districts                       |
| Fewer customers/intermediaries | personal selling, direct contact           |
| Also                           | e-commerce, reciprocity, varied contracts, |
|                                | complex financing, evolving relations      |
Problems: high complexity and uncertainty → risk (time, money, frustration);
several units involved with different interests.
*Interactive* purchasing: buyer and seller interdependent; the market is a
*network of relations* among organizations (Ford); long-term relations in a
complex network; every relation is affected by the whole network, not only the
dyad (Håkansson). Buyer (purchasing dept.) and sales manager each bring
objectives, resources, constraints, problem solving and people.

** The six phases
| # | Phase                                 | Type        |
|---+---------------------------------------+-------------|
| 1 | Specification definition              | strategic   |
| 2 | Supplier identification/qualification | strategic   |
| 3 | Selection and negotiation             | strategic   |
| 4 | Order issue                           | operational |
| 5 | Order monitoring (expediting)         | operational |
| 6 | Follow-up and supplier evaluation     | operational |
Steps are tightly linked: the output quality of each feeds the next — purchases
often exceed half the product's value, so *GIGO* (garbage in, garbage out).
Strategic phases involve more decision makers, need more information and
affect other units.
1. *Specifications*: characteristics (life, technical features), especially for
   custom items; medium/long-term requirements — direct materials from finished
   products through the bill of materials, indirect from historical data.
2. *Identification*: pre-selected list from internal (supplier database) and
   external information (internet, fairs, request for information); audits or
   questionnaires → *register of qualified suppliers*. Walmart lets suppliers
   apply on an online portal. Supplier hierarchy: potential → approved/qualified →
   preferred (pre-qualification, evaluation criteria, ranking → vendor list).
3. *Selection*: requests for quotation; evaluate offers with [[id:vendor-rating][vendor grid]],
   [[id:total-cost-of-ownership][TCO]], AHP; plus make-or-buy and supplier location. *Negotiation* fixes
   cost, delivery, penalties; AI increasingly simulates buyer behaviour.
4. *Order*: supplier accepts and confirms delivery date and quantity changes.
5. *Monitoring*: expediting (follow and chase progress), inspection visits,
   order tracking (supplier sends production/logistics progress, like a parcel
   tracker).
6. *Post-purchase*: complaints, assistance, spare parts, archive updates;
   evaluation of supplier performance → corrective actions. *BLM* shares its
   production status with suppliers on a portal and monitors their orders in
   real time, with shared KPIs on collaboration quality.

* Example
A machine builder needs a new laser source: it writes specifications from the
BOM, finds five suppliers at a fair, audits three, requests quotes, rates them
with a vendor grid and TCO, negotiates penalties for late delivery, issues the
order, tracks it weekly and finally scores on-time delivery and defects.

* Pitfall
Purchasing is not just ordering: the value is decided in phases 1–3. Derived
demand means a B2B buyer's volumes follow its customers' demand.
