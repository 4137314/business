#+title: Simple, functional, divisional and matrix structures
#+summary: The four consolidated structure models group work by entrepreneur, input (function), output (division) or both (matrix); each trades efficiency, effectiveness and coordination differently.

* Model
| Structure       | Grouping criterion       | Decentralization   | Typical case |
|-----------------+--------------------------+--------------------+--------------|
| Simple          | the owner                | none               | small firm   |
| Functional      | input: function          | limited, selective | Apple        |
| Multidivisional | output: product, region, | strong, parallel   | GE           |
| (divisional)    | customer, channel        | to divisions       |              |
| Matrix          | input and output         | power shared       | Nike         |

** 1. Simple structure
Small firms; directive and operating functions concentrated in the entrepreneur;
low task specialization and interchangeable roles; fast decisions and
operational flexibility. Chart: /Imprenditore/ over three boxes of /organi esecutivi/.

** 2. Functional (plurifunzionale) structure
Work divided horizontally by functional area (production, marketing,
administration, finance, personnel) — specialization by input; well developed
hierarchy; *selective* decentralization: strategic decisions, coordination and
control stay with top management, managerial and operating decisions go to
intermediate line managers.
Example: *Apple's* chart — under the CEO each senior vice-president leads a
function (hardware engineering, software engineering, marketing, operations,
retail), not a product line: the iPhone is made by all functions together.
| Advantages                          | Disadvantages                           |
|-------------------------------------+-----------------------------------------|
| efficiency of the whole system      | over-specialization hurts effectiveness |
| economies of scale                  | behaviour aimed at partial objectives   |
| specialist skills, steady quality   | line/line, line/staff conflicts         |
| operational flexibility             | managers' general skills and motivation |
| fast decisions and information flow | develop little                          |

** 3. Multidivisional structure
Activities grouped by main business segment (product lines, regions, customer
categories, distribution channels) into *divisions*; each division is internally
organized by function; strong, *parallel* decentralization towards divisions.
Three decision levels: corporate strategy (general management) → competitive
strategy per output (division heads, delegated) → managerial and operating
decisions (functions, delegated).
Example: *General Electric* — a corporate office with shared staff (legal,
research, business development) over business divisions such as aviation,
healthcare and energy, later separated into GE Aerospace, GE HealthCare and
GE Vernova. A textbook chart: pharmaceutical division, chemical division,
cosmetics division, each with its own R&D, production, sales, administration
and personnel.
| Advantages                              | Disadvantages                            |
|-----------------------------------------+------------------------------------------|
| favours effectiveness                   | penalizes global efficiency: duplicated  |
| manages technically, geographically and | specialist services and units → more     |
| commercially diversified activities     | resources, more capital → higher costs   |
| top management focuses on strategy,     | conflicts among divisions for resources, |
| control of common processes             | and between centre and divisions         |
| develops managers, motivation           | needs more coordination and control      |

** 4. Matrix structure
Typical of firms producing by project or to order. Cross-functional work: teams
of specialists from functions dedicated to a project/product/market. Each team
reports to the project/product manager, to the functional manager and to the
team leader. Two command lines: one on *resources and functions* (functional
directors), one on *results* (project directors).
Example: *Nike* — geographic divisions (North America, EMEA, Greater China,
Asia Pacific and Latin America) cross product categories (footwear, apparel,
equipment) and global functions (innovation and design, supply chain,
marketing, HR); brands Converse and Jordan sit alongside.
Advantages: efficiency and effectiveness, coordination of different activities
towards specific outputs, flexibility, diffusion of power. Disadvantages: sense
of precariousness and insecurity in staff; dual command can delay decisions.

** Combining criteria in practice
Real firms mix criteria on different levels: e.g. general management →
*functions* (production, sales, administration, personnel, research) → the sales
function split by *area* (North, Centre, South and islands) → each area split by
*product* (product 1, product 2), each with its own sales network.

* Worked example
A firm sells two very different product lines in three countries. Functional:
one factory, one sales team — cheapest, but each line competes for attention.
Divisional: two divisions with their own R&D and sales — each line responds to
its market, at the cost of duplicated staff. Matrix: shared engineers assigned
to line projects — scarce skills are reused, but every engineer has two bosses.
Choose by the interdependence that most needs tight coordination
([[id:contingency-theory][contingency]]).

* Pitfall
"Multidivisional" is not "many functions": divisions are defined by output.
Effectiveness vs efficiency is the key trade-off: functional optimizes
efficiency, divisional favours effectiveness, matrix tries both and pays in
conflicts. A chart shows formal reporting only, not processes or informal power.
