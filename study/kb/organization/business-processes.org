#+title: Processes and coordination
#+summary: The process logic groups activities into cross-functional key processes owned by a process owner and aimed at value perceived by the customer; coordination mechanisms reconnect divided work.

* Definition
A *process* is a logical aggregation/sequence of linked activities, performed in
different areas and responsibility centres, coordinated by a *key-process owner*
who has total responsibility for the final result:
\[ \text{Input} \longrightarrow \text{Activities} \longrightarrow \text{Output} \]
Processes are *transversal* to the formal structure drawn by the organization
chart. Example: new product development takes market needs and competitor data
as input, crosses R&D, engineering, sales and production, and delivers the
finished product as output.

* Model
** From functional logic to process logic
Market globalization, technological innovation and strong competition demand
dynamism, fast adaptation, innovation, flexibility and efficiency. The process
logic groups activities into *interfunctional key processes* according to their
contribution to the value perceived by the customer — the idea behind Business
Process Reengineering ([[id:improvement-approaches][BPR]]). In the process-based organization the customer
can be internal or external, the process owner acts as an "internal
entrepreneur", and resources are /persone di valore/ performing value-adding
activities, not people who merely perform activities.

| Process structure: advantages              | Process structure: limits             |
|--------------------------------------------+---------------------------------------|
| customer orientation, focus on             | hard to identify the right key        |
| activities giving competitive advantage    | processes                             |
| overall view of company goals, not of one  | needs deep change in culture, job     |
| function                                   | design and power arrangements         |
| teamwork, no internal boundaries, learning | low technical specialization, because |
| flexibility through delegation to the team | work is interfunctional               |
| fewer inefficiencies in work flows         |                                       |

** Coordination mechanisms
| Mechanism                      | What it fixes                                 |
|--------------------------------+-----------------------------------------------|
| Objectives                     | the result each unit must reach               |
| Plans and programmes           | what is done, when, with which resources      |
| Job descriptions (mansionari)  | /which/ tasks each position performs          |
| Procedures (norme procedurali) | /how/ the assigned tasks are performed        |
| Leadership styles              | how managers exercise power over subordinates |
Example of leadership style: Richard Branson described Steve Jobs as autocratic,
with a meticulous eye for detail, surrounding himself with like-minded people
(see [[id:motivation-and-leadership][Likert]]).

* Method
1. Name the customer and the output that customer values.
2. Map activities, hand-offs between functions, information and waiting.
3. Appoint a process owner with end-to-end responsibility.
4. Measure end-to-end time, quality, cost and reliability.
5. Improve the whole flow, not a single function's target.

* Example
Purchasing saves EUR 2 per unit by buying large batches; inventory holding and
obsolescence then cost EUR 3 per unit more. The function meets its target while
the order-to-delivery process becomes EUR 1 per unit worse — the typical
"partial objective" problem of the functional model that the process owner removes.

* Pitfall
A process organization does not abolish functions: specialist competence still
lives somewhere. Job descriptions say /what/, procedures say /how/ — do not swap them.

* Recall
Define process and process owner; list three advantages and the three limits of
the process structure; list the five coordination mechanisms.
